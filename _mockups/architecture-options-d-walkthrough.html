Day 1 walkthrough · v0.1
6 screens · current story

The current game's clarity problem is invisible state. Trust, defiance, compliance — the player can't see them, can't reason about them, can't tell if anything they did mattered. The fix is making everything visible: a day counter, a phase tracker with literal exit conditions, named NPC states instead of sliders, a journal, an inventory, and a list of endings still reachable. Below, six screens through Day 1 (and a glimpse of Day 5) show what this looks like.

01
Cold open

A frame, before anything else.

The first thing the player sees is the contract: how many days, what role, who decides at the end. The current game opens with an email and no scaffolding — the player has to discover what they're doing by trial and error.

Here, the rules of the place are stated in three lines. The player begins knowing what they're playing.

02
Day 1 opens

The tableau · and the state panel.

Four opportunity cards. Three tokens. One card is locked (a curiosity-gap — visible but not yet actionable). One is required.

On the right, the state panel. It's the player's source of truth, visible at all times: day counter, phase + exit condition, NPC states (no numbers), journal, inventory, and which endings are still reachable.

This sidebar is the single biggest fix for the clarity problem. The player can look at the world and understand what's true.

03
Mini-scene

Choices preview their outcomes.

The player picked Marcus's card. Scene fires. Three choices, each showing exactly what will change if you pick it — named state shifts, journal entries, unlocked or closed paths.

This is the central UX move. Instead of "+1 defiance" you see "Marcus → Wary but Interested" and "He'll bring you something dangerous on Day 4–5." The trade is legible before the commit.

Some previews show a path closing — real stakes, visible in advance.

Shift ends

A summary, in human terms.

Day 1 closes. The summary lists what you learned and where things stand — not "trust +0.1, defiance −0.2," but legible facts the player can reason about.

And critically: a one-sentence preview of tomorrow's tableau, so the player goes into Day 2 with an idea of what's coming. No mystery-box pacing — this is a clock game.

06
Four days later

Day 5: progression is visible.

Four shifts in. Compare this sidebar to Day 1's: NPC states have evolved, the journal carries five entries, Marcus's envelope is in inventory, and one ending has already closed (the dashed-out line through "Quiet Compliance").

The tableau now has 5 cards and only 3 tokens — real strategic choice. The player has to skip something. One of the cards is LLM-generated specifically because of the player's prior choices ("Marcus left an envelope on your desk overnight" only exists because the player asked the dangerous question on Day 1).

Progression is no longer something the player has to infer. It's drawn.

What this design fixes

From invisible state to legible progression.

The 14-day frame is visible from move one.

Title card states the contract. Sidebar shows day count and phase at all times. The player always knows how much game remains and what phase they're in.

States, not sliders.

Every quality becomes a named state. Wary but Interested, Increasingly Quiet, Calibrating. The player reads the world in human terms; the engine still tracks the numbers underneath.

Choices preview their consequences.

Before commit, the player sees the named state change, the journal entry that will be added, and any endings that close. The trade is legible before the pick.

Persistent record. Cumulative weight.

The journal, inventory, and ending list grow visibly across shifts. By Day 5, the player can see what they've done. Progression is no longer something to infer.

Phase exit conditions are stated.

"Phase 1 closes when: first session ✓ AND first report filed." No mystery-box pacing. The player can target the things that move them forward.

Each day previews the next.

The shift-end summary tells the player what tomorrow brings, in one sentence. The game's rhythm is a clock the player can read, not a fog they have to feel their way through.
