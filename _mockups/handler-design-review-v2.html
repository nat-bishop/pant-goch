§ 01 · The pivot

What v2 retired,
and what it kept.

v1 (Smallville Inbox) was a chat-driven correspondence game with six LLM agents, free-text composition, and a Director picking beats. Tonally on-brand, but the design loop sprawled. v2 (HANDLER) collapses the surface area to a single verb. Mechanics move out of the LLM and into authored YAML. The LLM never decides outcomes again.

Below: what got cut and what got rebuilt — side by side. The right column is the entire shipping contract for MVP.

v.1 — Smallville Inbox · retired

Chat correspondence with six agents

  • Six LLM agents: chat, classifier, extractor, summary, fact-checker, director
  • Free-text composition — player writes, Aria replies
  • Director LLM picks one beat per tick from eligible set
  • Player canon extraction from open-ended replies
  • Fact-checker retry loop guarding beat must-include claims
  • History compaction processor on the chat agent
  • Continuity Program Frankenstein scenario
  • Scene vs. one-shot beat distinction with exit_when
→
v.2 — HANDLER · MVP scope

Authorize one option, per cycle, until resolved

  • Three LLM agents: SoP synthesizer, drafter, dispatch renderer — none conversational
  • No composition — player picks one of 3–4 drafted options
  • No Director — beats fire when their predicates fire; player picks from drafted set
  • No player canon — no free-text input means none needed
  • No fact-checker — facts aren't a state primitive; tags are
  • No compaction — three short, stateless calls per cycle
  • Op. LYNX-Exfil: two agents, six cycles, one authored moment
  • All beats predicate-gated; fires_at_most controls re-firing
§ 02 · The verb

One word, load-bearing.

Every player input in HANDLER is a single click. No typing. No multi-step composition. No agent-by-agent micromanagement. Each cycle, Operations Desk drafts 3–4 candidate courses of action; each option is a short paragraph naming an agent, an action, and an honest description of the cost. The player reads, picks, clicks. The cycle resolves.

authorize
— to formally permit a course of action proposed by Operations.
verb   /ˈɔːθəraɪz/   · transitive · cycle-binding
Anti-pattern · do not implement

No free-text composition. No multi-step decisions per turn. No agent-by-agent micromanagement. Choice over composition — the player picks; they do not write briefs.

§ 03 · The cycle

Three LLM calls,
one click.

Every cycle has the same shape: a forward-looking memo, a candidate option set, a player decision, and a field dispatch in voice. The LLM never decides outcomes — those are computed from the chosen option's tags by the engine. The LLM writes prose conditioned on resolved state.

i.
LLM · Operations voice

State of Play

Synthesizes the case forward. Names the threat, the resources, what hasn't moved. 1–3 paragraphs.

ii.
LLM · Operations voice

Options drafted

3–4 candidates tagged with (archetype, agent, params), each a paragraph honestly telegraphing cost.

iii.
Player · authorize

One click

Read, reason, pick. No typing. No undo. The decision binds the cycle and accretes into the campaign.

iv.
Engine + LLM · agent voice

Effects · dispatch

Engine applies authored effects deterministically. Renderer files a field dispatch in the acting agent's register.

One cycle, one cost. Every authorization consumes exactly one of cycles_remaining. Multi-cycle consequences are modeled as authored beats with cycle-delta predicates — never as variable-length actions. The economy is honest because the cost is uniform.
§ 04 · State model

Three numbers
and an inbox.

The entire visible UI surface for state is three trackable numbers plus the inbox of past dispatches. No heat meter. No hypothesis bars. No political capital track. Pressure is felt through cover decay — driven by passive beats — and the cycle countdown.

State is qualities — typed, namespaced fields with set/add/max/min effects gated by predicates. There is no other state primitive. Cover at zero is the loss state for that agent — no separate alive flag. Stage 3 fires the win terminal. cycles_remaining at zero fires the timeout terminal.

Cover · the exposure meter Integer 0–10 per agent. Decremented by aggressive archetypes and by passive pressure when the mission isn't progressing. At zero, the agent is lost.
Cycles · the turn budget Authored per mission (LYNX-Exfil: six). Decrements at cycle close. Reaches zero → timeout failure terminal.
Stage · the win track Mission-authored. LYNX-Exfil uses 0–3 (papers, location, exfil). Reaching the cap fires the win terminal.

§ 05 · The three primitives

Three kinds of thing
that can happen.

Archetypes are the dynamic moment-to-moment menu. Moments are the rare authored decision points where consequence is permanent. Beats are the silent reactions and terminals. Every event in HANDLER fits into exactly one of these three categories.

PRIM · 01
Archetype
The drafter's menu

An authored action template — identifier, when predicate, effect template, optional params. The drafter selects from the engine-computed set of valid archetypes each cycle and writes prose for 3–4 of them.

Example: RUN_EXFIL, valid at stage 2, drops LYNX cover by 3, advances stage to 3.

Fires bydrafter selection
Player choice?yes — one of several
For LYNX~7 archetypes
PRIM · 02
Moment
The forced decision

An authored option-shape the engine forces the drafter to include when its trigger predicate fires. Stage directions, not example text — author specifies the dramatic function, LLM writes situated prose at fire-time.

Walking-Dead-style "Carley or Doug" — permanent, consequential, named without literal text.

Fires byengine, forces drafter
Player choice?yes — but the slot is mandated
For LYNX1 moment (kestrel_sacrifice)
PRIM · 03
Beat
The silent reaction

A predicate-gated engine event with no player input. Three roles: terminals (win/loss), reaction effects (mark-as-lost), and passive pressure (cover decay when not progressing).

Beats just fire when their predicates are satisfied. No Director, no scheduler.

Fires bypredicate evaluation
Player choice?no — passive engine event
For LYNX5 beats (3 terminal · 1 react · 1 passive)
§ 06 · LLM contracts

Three calls.
Each writes only prose.

The load-bearing contract across all three: the LLM never decides game outcomes. Outcomes are computed deterministically from option tags by the engine. The LLM's job is exclusively prose. If a dispatch contradicts the resolved state delta, that's a bug in the prompt — not a different outcome.

Call 01 · synthesizer
State of Play memo
Operations Desk · terse, declarative, forward-looking
Instate · last 2–3 dispatches · mission framing
Out1–3 paragraphs in Operations voice
Whencycle open
Constraint: looks forward, no options proposed, no outcome prediction. Names the threat, the resources, what hasn't moved.
Call 02 · drafter
Options drafted
Operations Desk · candidates with honest costs
Instate · valid archetypes · dossiers · SoP · any forced moment
Out3–4 options as tags + prose
Whenafter SoP, before player
Constraint: tags MUST reference valid archetypes. Prose MUST telegraph cost honestly and MUST NOT predict outcomes. Tonal range required — at least one safe-but-slow, one fast-but-risky.
Call 03 · renderer
Dispatch renderer
Acting agent · authored register, first-person
Inauthorized option · state delta · agent dossier · recent dispatches
Out1–4 paragraphs in acting agent voice
Whenafter effects apply
Constraint: MUST honor the resolved state delta. MUST NOT introduce facts inconsistent with state. For HOLD, dispatch is in Operations voice (no acting agent).
Truth contract · across all three

The LLM never writes state. The engine never writes prose. Outcomes are computed from option tags — not narrated into being.

§ 07 · UI directions · three options

Same cycle.
Three philosophies.

Below: the same authorize screen — cycle 4 of LYNX-Exfil, the four options from Appendix A of the spec — rendered three different ways. Each direction commits to a different answer to a single question: what is the player actually doing when they click?

Click each tab to switch. Hover and click the options inside each viewport — they all work.

The player is opening a folder.

Direction A leans into the v.0.2 mockup's visual language. The authorize screen is a desk surface — warm paper documents laid out on dark obsidian, classified stamps, typewritten field reports, rotated slightly as if dropped. State is rendered on a stained legal pad. The four options are loose typed slips.

Tonally: slow, tactile, character-driven. It says the player is reviewing case material, not operating software. Closest in spirit to Tinker Tailor Soldier Spy — the bureau as physical place.

Tradeoffs
Hottest aesthetic — most distinctive, most memorable.
Honors v.0.2 concept — the visual research is already done.
Paper-as-metaphor reinforces the "no undo" weight of authorization.
Costliest to build — texture, rotation, layered shadows everywhere.
Accessibility risk — low-contrast warm tones, italicized body prose.
Can feel twee if executed unevenly — props need to feel earned.

The player is at a console.

Direction B answers the same question with a phosphor CRT. Monospace from corner to corner. The state block is a bordered ASCII frame; options are a numbered list under a section header; authorization is a command prompt at the bottom. Scanlines, vignette, the occasional blink.

Tonally: cold, technical, operational. The bureau as 1973 ops center. Closest in spirit to The Sandbaggers control room or Slow Horses' Slough House terminal sessions.

Tradeoffs
Cheapest to build — flat, monospace, no textures, no rotations.
Maximum information density without feeling cluttered.
Reinforces "the LLM is a system you query" framing.
Risk of trope — every spy game has a green terminal already.
Phosphor monoculture can flatten the dispatch agents' distinct voices.
Less memorable as a screenshot than Direction A.

The player is reading a page.

Direction C strips the UI to its quietest possible form. The authorize screen is a single page of a literary spy novel — drop cap on the State of Play, the three numbers as a small folio strip, the options as numbered prose paragraphs separated by ornamental dividers. No HUD chrome. No color beyond ink and a single burnt-orange accent.

Tonally: writerly, contemplative, prestige-game. The bureau as fiction. Closest in spirit to Suzerain or 80 Days — reading-experience-first, mechanics audible only when you reach for them.

Tradeoffs
Most likely to make the prose-led MVP feel worth reading.
Highest accessibility — large serif type, high contrast, no rotations.
Translates cleanly to mobile / Kindle / read-aloud.
Weakest game-feel — feels like an IF parser, not a spy console.
State numbers compete with prose for player attention.
Hardest to grow into UI for terminals / outcome cards / restart loops.

§ 08 · Comparison

How the three
diverge.

Each direction makes a different commitment. The matrix below picks the dimensions that matter for an MVP whose verb is authorize: what the player thinks they're doing, how prose lands, how state registers, and how much it costs to build well.

A · Dossier B · Ops Terminal C · Field Edition
Player mental model Reviewing case material. Operating a console. Reading a chapter.
Voice carrier Typewritten memos, props, stamps. Plain monospace text. Justified serif body prose.
State legibility Medium — pip bars on rotated paper. High — ASCII bars, big numerals. Low-medium — spelled out, quieter.
Prose foregrounding Strong — but competes with chrome. Weak — monospace flattens voice. Strongest — typography is the UI.
Build cost High — textures, rotations, shadows. Low — flat, monospace, no assets. Medium — typography discipline.
Scales to terminal / outcome card Excellent — final dispatch as memo. Excellent — final readout fits the frame. Mediocre — outcome card needs new language.
Tonal risk Twee if uneven. Generic if not careful. Inert — feels like reading, not playing.
Closest reference Tinker Tailor Soldier Spy (1979) The Sandbaggers / Slow Horses Suzerain · 80 Days
Studio recommendation · for review

Ship Dossier for MVP. Keep Field Edition's typography discipline as a constraint.

The verb is authorize; the player needs to feel the weight of a decision they cannot take back. Paper-as-metaphor carries that weight in a way a terminal does not — folders close, stamps stay stamped. Direction A also extends most naturally to the outcome card (final dispatch as a closing memo) and to future content (a new mission is a new folder).

That said, Direction C's typography discipline — generous measure, a single accent, serif body — should be a hard constraint on Direction A's body prose. The risk in Direction A is that it decorates instead of communicating; Direction C's restraint is the antidote. If the props don't earn their place, they get cut.

Direction B is the right fallback if build time compresses. Phosphor terminals carry the same information without any custom assets, and the mechanics-honest framing fits HANDLER's "LLM never decides" contract. Recommend revisiting after one playtest of A.
