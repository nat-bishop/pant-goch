Adding a spatial layer to a storylet engine.

4 candidate approaches

The real question is verb count, not architecture.

Great narrative-mechanics games are deliberate about how many things the player can do. Her Story has one verb. Reigns has one. 80 Days has three. Disco Elysium has four. Adding a verb is a tax — it takes design oxygen, complicates onboarding, and competes with everything else for the player's attention.

So the right framing isn't "how do I bolt a map onto my engine." It's: does adding a spatial verb earn its keep, and if so, what is the cleanest way to make it first-class instead of a sidecar?

The answer depends on whether spatial navigation is doing real work — creating decisions, legibility, mutual exclusion — or just rearranging the inbox into rooms.

The map is worth adding only if "where am I" creates a decision the player wouldn't otherwise face. If every room always has exactly one queued beat, the map is a menu in a costume.

Data model

Authoring
# scenario.yaml
beats:
  - id: witness_arrival
    npc: witness
    when:
      - AtLocation: lobby
      - Not_:
          BeatFired: witness_arrival

  - id: archive_search
    npc: witness
    when:
      - AtLocation: archive
      - QualityGte:
          key: trust
          v: 0.4

locations:
  - id: lobby
    name: "Lobby"
  - id: archive
    name: "Archive"
    unlock_when:
      - QualityGte:
          key: access_level
          v: 1
Player experience
  1. Map UI shows 3 unlocked rooms; player clicks Lobby.
  2. Engine ticks with location=lobby. Eligibility query: any beat whose at_location predicate is satisfied — gets witness_arrival. Beat fires; chat agent renders the scene.
  3. Player replies; classifier & extractor run as today. Tick advances. Player picks the next room. "Same as the email game, with a room icon attached."

Strengths

  • Cheapest delta. 5-edit predicate pattern, encoder & drift suite extend trivially.
  • Engine logic unchanged. No risk of regressing today's eligibility behaviour.
  • Mixable. Inbox-style beats stay first-class — just omit at_location.

Weaknesses

  • Map is decorative. The data model has no notion of a room; rooms are string labels on a flat list.
  • Authoring overhead per beat. Every spatial beat needs at_location repeated; locations don't own their beats.
  • NPC presence is implicit. Marcus being "in the lounge today" is encoded across N beat predicates instead of one schedule.
Closest in spirit Fallen London (storylet predicates) Cultist Simulator (free verb selection)
B
Approach — structural
Locations as first-class containers
Locations become a sibling of dossiers. Beats live inside a location (or are flagged "floating" for inbox-style content). NPCs gain a presence schedule. Eligibility partitions by location first, predicates within.

Data model
Locations own their beats; NPCs have presence schedules pointing at one location per shift. "Floating" beats remain unlocated — the inbox is preserved as a content surface.
Authoring
locations:
  - id: lobby
    name: "Lobby"
    beats:
      - id: witness_arrival
        when: [{Not_: {BeatFired: witness_arrival}}]
      - id: small_talk
        kind: scene
        exit_when: [{QualityGte: {key: trust, v: 0.3}}]

  - id: archive
    name: "Archive"
    unlock_when: [{QualityGte: ...}]
    beats: [...]

npcs:
  - id: witness
    presence:
      - {shift: 1, at: lobby}
      - {shift: 2, at: archive}

floating_beats:        # inbox-style
  - id: welcome_email
  - id: cpo_notice
Player experience
  1. Player sees three unlocked rooms; the witness's icon is shown in the Lobby. Visits Lobby.
  2. Engine partitions: candidate beats = lobby.beats ∪ floating; predicates evaluated within. Witness scene fires.
  3. Mid-conversation, player walks out. The scene is interrupted (configurable: pause vs. abort). Floating CPO notice is still readable from anywhere. "Marcus moved. The room is empty now."

Strengths

  • Authoring matches fiction. A location's content reads as a coherent unit; NPCs as positioned entities.
  • Reduces predicate boilerplate. No at_location chip on every beat — partition does the work.
  • Encoder gets simpler. Per-location reachability is more structured than predicate string-matching.
  • Floating beats preserve the inbox. You don't lose async correspondence as a content type.

Weaknesses

  • Two-tier eligibility. Spine logic and encoder both need a coordinated change; drift suite is the safety net.
  • NPC schedules are new authoring. Light, but you must author "where Marcus is when".
  • Scene-on-leave semantics needs a decision. Pause? Abort with an effect? Re-enter to resume? — one explicit rule, not author-by-author.
Closest in spirit Citizen Sleeper (locations as drift targets) Pentiment (rooms with NPC schedules) Suzerain (offices as content containers)
C
Approach — maximalist
Scene-graph as the world model
Locations are nodes in a graph; edges are walkable connections. Movement is a real verb with cost. The inbox is no longer a parallel surface — it's content at the "your_desk" node. Storylets become events that fire when state × location matches.

Data model
The world is a connected graph. Movement is a verb; you can only travel along an edge. Each node has a content surface (NPCs present, items, queued storylets).
Authoring
locations:
  - id: office
    name: "Your Office"
    connects_to: [hallway, lobby]
    surfaces:
      - type: inbox          # desk has email
      - type: examine
        target: desk_drawer

  - id: hallway
    connects_to: [office, archive, stair]

  - id: archive
    connects_to: [hallway, records]
    unlock_edge:
      - to: records
        when: [{HasItem: archive_key}]

storylets:
  - id: witness_in_archive
    at: archive
    when: [{NpcPresent: witness}]
    trigger: on_enter     # or on_action
Player experience
  1. Player is at office. Map shows three reachable nodes; roof is visible but the door icon is red.
  2. Player walks: office → hallway → archive (3 movement actions, or one if travel is free between connected nodes). On entering archive, an on_enter storylet fires because the witness is present.
  3. Storylet ends. Player examines a shelf at the archive node, finds archive_key. The hallway→records edge unlocks; the door icon turns green. "The map is the game. Walking is meaningful."

Strengths

  • Strongest spatial legibility. Locked edges, NPC presence, and discoverable items show the world's shape.
  • Multiple verbs without bloat. Walk, examine, talk — each does narrative work; together they make a real grammar.
  • Items, keys, presence become design tools. "Find the key to unlock the records room" is now expressible as content, not predicates.

Weaknesses

  • Largest engine refactor. Movement, edges, on_enter triggers, surface types — meaningfully more code than B.
  • The inbox stops being primary. If async correspondence is core to the experience, you'd be demoting it.
  • Authoring time per location grows. Connections + surfaces + items + presence = more knobs.
  • Risk of becoming a parser-IF. Without restraint you re-invent Inform 7 and lose the storylet engine's compactness.
Closest in spirit Sorcery! (Inkle) Disco Elysium Pentiment Norco
D
Approach — orthogonal
Skip space — opportunity board instead
Drop the spatial metaphor. The new verb is allocate, not walk. Each shift, the player has N action tokens to spend on a tableau of available opportunities (some scheduled, some triggered, some hidden). Authoring stays flat; the strategic layer comes from token scarcity.

modelflat + cost
Data model
Per-shift token budget. Opportunities surface based on state. Choosing where tokens go is the strategic verb.
Authoring
opportunities:
  - id: briefing_with_director
    cost: 1
    surface_when:
      - ShiftEq: 2
    triggers: director_briefing_beat

  - id: interview_witness
    cost: 2          # big commitment
    surface_when:
      - QualityGte:
          key: trust
          v: 0.3
    triggers: witness_interview_scene

  - id: archive_search
    cost: 1
    surface_when: [...]
    triggers: archive_search_beat

shift_config:
  tokens_per_shift: 3
  unspent_tokens: discard
  end_shift_when: tokens_exhausted
                       | player_chooses
Player experience
  1. Shift opens. Player sees 4 opportunity cards on a tableau, with token costs visible. Has 3 tokens.
  2. Player drags a token onto "interview the witness" (cost 2, big commitment). Beat fires immediately or queues; chat agent renders the scene.
  3. 1 token remains. Player picks the cheap option, ending the shift. Tomorrow's tableau will look different. "What do I spend on, and what do I let pass?"

Strengths

  • Strategic depth without spatial cost. Token scarcity is the decision; no map UI to design or navigate.
  • Lightest engine change. Beats stay as they are; opportunities are a thin selection layer above them.
  • Fits a Severance-flat aesthetic. A schedule, not a building. Cold and corporate.
  • Variable cost — expensive opportunities create real trade-offs.

Weaknesses

  • Loses spatial curiosity. No "what's behind the locked door" gap — the locked card is visible but flat.
  • Less embodied fiction. The world feels like a UI, not a place — whether that's good depends on the story.
  • Token economy needs careful tuning. Wrong cost → trivial choices; right cost is sensitive.
Closest in spirit Citizen Sleeper (drives + dice) Cultist Simulator (verb tableau) Reigns (one-card-at-a-time)

Three questions — answer in order

These resolve the choice without doing more architecture work. They're about what the game is, not how it's built.

1
Is the world being explored itself a character — a place the player should learn the shape of?
Yes → B
2
Do you want walking, examining, and item-finding as real verbs — or is the goal still primarily conversation with NPCs?
Walk → C
3
If you only need strategic decisions per shift — not embodied space — would a token budget do the work without a map?
Yes → D
Default recommendation

Start with B. Reach for C only if walking-as-verb earns its keep. Keep D in mind as the "honest" alternative.

B is the structural fit: it respects the fiction without over-committing the engine. Locations as containers + NPC presence schedules give you the strategic and curiosity layers a map provides, without taking on movement-as-state. If after prototyping you find yourself wanting connected paths, locked doors, and physical items, that's the signal to lift to C. D is worth genuinely considering if the answer to question 1 is "no" — sometimes the most elegant move is to admit the map was a metaphor for action allocation, and just build the allocation.
