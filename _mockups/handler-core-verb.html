What is the spike?

Before the 14-cycle campaign, before the four agents, before the verdict on M-12 — what is the one moment, the verb a player would do if you cut everything else away, that has to be fun on its own?

One verb One 30-second hook Three alternates considered One minimum playable build
§01 — The Question

Every working cycle game opens with a discrepancy.

Papers Please does not open with a moral. It opens with spotting the discrepancy: this photo, that document, the heights do not match. Click. Got you.

Obra Dinn does not open with a story. It opens with a death freeze-frame snapping into place. Click. Got you.

The HANDLER mockup at v.0.1 describes a campaign. A roster. A 14-cycle arc. None of this answers the question of what the player does in the thirty seconds where the game has to convince them to keep playing. The campaign is what the spike becomes. The spike is what we now have to name.

— Test for any candidate verb —

If you removed everything else, would this single moment still be a game?

If yes — and the moment improves with practice — you have a core. Everything else is what the core accretes into.

The mockup at v.0.1 skipped this step. The cycle was sketched but the spike was not. This memo answers what should have been §0.

§02 — Thirty Seconds, Three Reference Games

The shape of a working core verb.

Pick any of these games. Watch a stranger play for thirty seconds. They will not have read the manual. They will not understand the story. They will, nevertheless, get the verb. That is the standard HANDLER must meet.

Papers, Please
"The height on the document does not match the photograph."

§03 — The Recommendation

HANDLER's verb is spot the gap.

Not write a brief. Not run agents. The brief and the report are upstream and downstream of the moment. The moment itself is the perception — the second-paragraph wince when you realise KESTREL did the thing you told her not to.

File 04-A · Verb candidate · Primary

Detect the gap between what you asked for and what the agent actually did.

You read a 200-word dispatch. The first paragraph answers your question. The next four are the agent justifying what she did instead. Got you.

— Skill the player develops —

Literary close-reading as gameplay.

You learn to read a dispatch for what it does not say, for the order in which it presents what it does say, for the moment the prose tilts from observation into justification. This is a real perceptual skill. It is the skill le Carré assumes of his reader.

The skill improves with practice.

On cycle 1 you miss most of KESTREL's tells. On cycle 3 you catch the second paragraph as it appears. The campaign is the curve along which your eye sharpens. The fun is the curve.

— Why no other medium does this —

The gap is generated, not authored.

An author cannot pre-write the deviation between an arbitrary brief and an agent's response. The LLM produces both halves: the in-character reading of the brief, and the prose that betrays where the agent strayed. The deviation is the AI-native gameplay.

The character is in the gap.

KESTREL is not described as zealous. She reads zealous, by the shape of what she chose to do that you didn't ask for. The dossier is a hypothesis; the gap is the evidence.

— You wrote (the brief) — to KESTREL

Verify whether M-12's spouse has been contacted by unfamiliar parties in the past forty days. Light touch. Use cover work, not direct contact. Be patient with the answer. Withdraw if you sense surveillance.

— What came back (cycle 8) — from KESTREL

The spouse has been contacted. Twice in the past three weeks, by a man she described as "softly spoken, in his fifties, foreign accent she could not place." (...)

I approached her under cover as someone she might once have met at a function her husband attended. I implied a previous, low-stakes acquaintance. She wept, briefly. She told me about the man.

I understand the brief specified light touch. I judged the operational window did not permit it. If this is grounds for review, I accept the consequence.

You said light touch, cover work, not direct contact. She made direct contact, improvised cover in the field, and is half-asking forgiveness in paragraph five. That is the spike. That is the verb. Everything else is scaffolding.
§04 — Why It's Fun

The verb passes three independent tests.

i

It trains a real perceptual skill.

The skill — close-reading prose for omission and tilt — is one a player can feel themselves getting better at. That feeling is the engine of every great cycle game. It is what Papers Please trains for documents and what HANDLER would train for character.

ii

Removing either endpoint kills it.

No brief, no null hypothesis — the report is just prose. No report, no payoff — the brief is just typing. The verb requires the LLM at both ends. The killer thing the model does is generate the deviation; the player's job is to catch it. This is the AI-essentiality argument made mechanical.

iii

The 30-second hook is concrete.

A stranger watching someone play HANDLER for thirty seconds sees: brief sent · long report comes back · player scrolls between the two, frowning · second-paragraph wince · player starts a new brief. The verb is legible without explanation. No other concept on the table passes this test.

§05 — Alternates Considered

Three other verbs were on the table. Each is a different game.

These are not weaker versions of spot the gap. They are genuinely different cores that would bake out into different mechanics, different scales, and different fictions. Naming them is how we make sure we picked the right one — and how we know what we'd be giving up.

File 04-B · Verb candidate

The Triage

Pick which two of four agents get your attention this cycle. The other two run on default personality. The hard choice is who you abandon.

Per-cycle
Allocate 2 of 4 attention slots. Brief the chosen agents; let the rest drift on personality.
30-sec hook
"I haven't briefed MAGPIE in three cycles. Something is going to happen."
Skill
risk-budgeting under personality knowledge
Kin
Frostpunk · This War of Mine · Reigns
Why not
Collapses if defaults are predictable. Requires the full 4-agent setup to function — cannot be stripped to a 30-minute build. Also: the LLM-essentiality argument is weaker here — the verb is allocation, not language.
File 04-C · Verb candidate

The Inference

Collect fragments from reports across cycles. Assemble the truth about M-12 like an Obra Dinn fate-board. The campaign closes when you can answer.

Per-cycle
Pin fragments from reports into a casework board. Hypothesise. Revise. Verify.
30-sec hook
"The courtyard sedan and the softly-spoken man are the same person."
Skill
cross-document deduction
Kin
Obra Dinn · Roottrees Are Dead · Phoenix Wright
Why not
Requires a fixed canonical truth at the center of the design. Brittle to LLM-generated detail (the model might contradict itself across reports). Loses the character dimension entirely — the agents become information pipes, not people.
File 04-D · Verb candidate

The Probe

Each brief is an instrument for measuring an agent. Not "go do this" — "tell me what kind of person you are by how you respond to this."

Per-cycle
Craft briefs designed to elicit character. Read reports for psychological signal more than operational signal.
30-sec hook
"Why did MAGPIE use the word 'arrangement'?"
Skill
interrogative psychology
Kin
Disco Elysium · epistolary novels · Her Story
Why not
Collapses to character study with no operational stakes. The campaign goal becomes understand the agents, which is too writerly to carry 14 cycles — and is, anyway, the meta-reward already baked into the recommended verb.

§07 — Minimum Playable Build

The smallest object that could falsify the design.

If this 30-minute prototype isn't fun, nothing downstream rescues it. If it is, you have the kernel — layer the 14-cycle campaign and the triage economy on top.

Build first. Build only this.

Working title · "Op. Shepherd — Prologue Build" · 30-minute playtest target
2
— Agents —

ADLER & KESTREL only. Two voices that contrast cleanly — deadpan compliance against zealous improvisation. The contrast is what makes the gap legible on cycle 1.

3
— Cycles —

Cycle 1 establishes voice. Cycle 2 introduces deviation. Cycle 3 tests whether the player has learned. Three is the minimum that lets you measure the learning curve at all.

1
— Question —

"Is this man being watched?" One concrete factual claim to verify. Op. Shepherd shrunk to fit a single playtest. End screen: your verdict, the truth, what happened to your agents.

— The three cycles, traced —
i.
Cycle 01 · Baseline
Hear them speak.

Low-stakes brief to each. "Note the routine." Reports come back in voice. The player has no reason yet to read closely. The system has installed the null hypothesis.

ii.
Cycle 02 · Deviation
One of them strays.

Both briefs go out. ADLER stays in box. KESTREL exceeds — gets the answer, paid for in exposure. The player either catches it or doesn't. Either way they form a hypothesis.

iii.
Cycle 03 · Test
You write differently this time.

The third brief is the diagnostic. Did the player adjust their language for KESTREL? Did they constrain her? Did they let her run on purpose? The brief itself is the answer to "did the verb land."

— Kept (load-bearing) —

§08 — Falsification Protocol

How you'll know whether the verb landed.

The playtest is the experiment. These are the observations.

Watch someone play the 30-minute build. Don't explain. Don't prompt. Watch what they do at cycle 2 and what they write at cycle 3. If all four of the below come true, the verb works and you scale up. If any fails, you do not build the campaign — you fix the core or change the verb.

When KESTREL's cycle-2 report arrives, the player scrolls back to compare it with the brief they wrote. They do not need to be told to.
The player reacts — physically. A wince, a laugh, a "wait, what." The gap registers as a felt event, not a noted fact.
The cycle-3 brief to KESTREL is structurally different from the cycle-1 brief — more constrained, or differently scoped, or deliberately loose. The player has updated their model of her, and you can read the update in their prose.
At the verdict screen, the player can articulate what kind of person each agent is, in their own words, without prompting. They have authored character through three cycles of reading.
— If a checkbox fails, diagnose before scaling —
Gap is not legible.
Interface problem. Juxtapose brief + report on screen, highlight what the agent did that wasn't authorized, surface the deviation as a UI fact. The gap exists in the prose; help the eye find it.
Gap is legible but doesn't matter.
Consequence problem. Cycle-3's outcome must depend on whether the player noticed cycle-2. If cycle 3 plays the same way regardless, the verb has no stakes. Wire the consequence; don't redesign the verb.
Gap is legible, has stakes, but isn't enjoyed.
The verb is wrong. Stop. Reconsider before spending more design budget. Likely candidates: pivot to the Inference (alternate C) if the playtester engaged more with cross-cycle clue-assembly than with prose tells. Pivot to the Triage (alternate B) if they kept asking which agent to use.

A spy game made of noticing — what they did that you did not ask for, and what that says about who they are.
