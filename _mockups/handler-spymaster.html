Handler.

An intelligence game made of briefs and reports
You write what they should do. They go and do something. You read about what happened.
One verb Two LLM endpoints Four agents in the field 14 cycles to commit
§01 — Premise

You don't go into the field. They do.

You sit at a desk in a room without windows. Your agents are out there. You will never meet them. You communicate only through written briefs that travel through dead drops and encrypted channels — and they reply only through reports that arrive on your desk between cycles.

Each cycle, you write one brief per agent. One sentence, one paragraph, one page — the format is yours. They go and execute, off-screen. The cycle ends. Their reports arrive: short prose dispatches, generated by the LLM, conditioned on what you wrote, who they are, and what's actually out there.

Some reports come back exactly as you'd hoped. Some don't come back at all. Some come back longer than they should be, with the agent justifying what they did in your name. Over fourteen cycles, you learn what each agent does with your words. Your eye sharpens. Some agents you stop trusting. Some you trust more than you should.

— Operating Premise —

The handler is judged by the cumulative weight of his decisions, not by any single one.

A bold instruction may produce a catastrophe or a miracle. A cautious one may produce nothing at all. The handler who learns the temperament of his agents is the handler who survives. The handler who does not is replaced.

§02 — The Cycle

One verb. Repeated until the campaign closes.

Every cycle is the same shape, regardless of mission. Five stages, two of them belonging to the LLM, two to the player, one to the world.

The handler's cycle.

§05 — Why the LLM

The model occupies both ends of the loop.

Most "AI in games" attempts use the LLM at one point — usually rendering NPC dialogue. Here the LLM does two essential jobs back-to-back: interpret a free-text instruction in character, then generate the prose dispatch that comes back. Neither end can be authored.

An author can't write every brief in advance.

The player writes arbitrary natural language. "Eliminate the asset" reads differently from "neutralise the asset by sundown unless he's already dead." Different agents read each differently. No menu, no decision tree, no authored content covers this combinatorial space.

An author can't write every report.

Each dispatch is uniquely shaped by the brief, the agent's personality, the world state, the outcome, and what the agent chose to omit. A 200-word ADLER report and a 500-word KESTREL report on the same outcome are different texts. The LLM produces both in voice.

The agent's misinterpretation is content.

When KESTREL exceeds her brief, the LLM is generating the act and the justification together. The handler's relationship to each agent's voice becomes the depth of the game. This is the killer thing the LLM does: character at scale, in writing.

The same brief, twice, is a different game.

Stochastic generation means even an identical brief to an identical agent on an identical day produces a meaningfully different report. Patterns persist (KESTREL is always KESTREL); particulars don't. Replays are real plays, not the same path with different cosmetics.

§06 — Across a Campaign

Fourteen cycles. Things accrete.

A single cycle is a chamber piece. The campaign is the novel that emerges from fourteen of them. The handler's eye sharpens. Some agents don't make it. Some do, but you wish they hadn't.

§07 — Engine Architecture

The deterministic spine survives. The model fills two slots.

The current Smallville engine — predicate-gated beats, authored effects, drift-tested encoder — carries forward almost unchanged. Two new LLM jobs slot into a wider tick.

What stays

Authored world state

Agents have authored profiles (skills, weaknesses, loyalty range, voice register). Missions have authored objectives, outcome distributions, and possible complications. Quality fields drive predicate eligibility just as today.

Deterministic state writes

apply_literal_effects remains the only mutation path. Outcome resolution writes through it. Agent-state changes (loyalty drift, exposure increase, casualty) are authored effects gated by authored predicates.

Encoder & drift suite

The static analysis pipeline (ASP encoder + engine consistency tests) carries over. Mission graphs are reachable-encoded; the suite catches divergence between the spine and the encoder.

What's new

LLM Job 1 · Interpreter

brief_interpret(agent_profile, brief_text, world_snapshot) → an outcome modifier from a constrained schema (compliance, scope_drift, aggression, caution). The world's authored outcome distribution shifts based on these. Engine validates the output schema; rejection → retry → fall back to "neutral compliance."

LLM Job 2 · Dispatch renderer

render_dispatch(agent_voice, brief, outcome, world_state) → 100–600 words of prose, with optional fields (exposure_admitted, request_for_followup) extracted by a downstream classifier. Length, omissions, and tone are authored constraints; specific phrasings are LLM.

One new tick trigger

cycle_close fires after all dispatched briefs have been resolved and rendered. Reports populate the inbox. Predicates re-evaluate. The handler returns to the desk.

A spy game made of writing — where the only thing your agents have to hold against you is what you wrote, and the only thing you have to hold against them is what they wrote back.
