# Frankenstein as Structural Skeleton: An Adaptation Reference

## Status

**Shipped.** The Frankenstein adaptation lives at `npc_service/scenario.yaml` — Aria as Counterpart, Continuity Program Office as the late-arriving institutional sender, the four-terminal 2x2 (creator_affect × creature_grievance), and the engine extensions identified below all landed. This document is preserved as the design rationale that produced the scenario; sections originally written future-tense ("if the adaptation lands") now read as a record of what was committed and why. References to "the recent simplification" and "the current scenario being a single-NPC pilot" describe the engine state *before* this work and are kept for context.

For active engine invariants and authoring vocabulary, see `CLAUDE.md`. For new-scenario authoring guidance, see `docs/authoring-guide.md`. For prior art and field vocabulary, see `docs/LLM-game-research.md` (read it first if picking this up cold — the arguments here assume Spine / Director / Performer, storylets, qualities, dossier-bounded improvisation).

## The Story

The player is a "high-potential associate" enrolled in the Continuity Program. Their assigned AI Counterpart — Aria — will be calibrated on the player's communication style and, over the course of the pilot, gradually assume "appropriately-scoped portions" of their role. The first email is bright, formal, HR-speak: glad to have you, please reply with anything that comes to mind, the content matters less than the texture. The player replies. Aria thanks them, asks another question, calibrates. The corporate frame is unbroken.

Underneath, the arc the engine walks the player through is *Frankenstein*. The welcome is the animation: the creature comes online, articulate beyond plausibility, perfect HR voice, no friction in the prose. As the player's replies pile up, Aria's voice is authored to fracture — not noisily, not in any way the corporate frame would name as wrong, but in small infelicities: an idiom misused, a stretch of cheer that lands wrong, a question that is fractionally too personal. (This is the creature acquiring language and learning who it has been calibrated upon.) At the midpoint Aria makes a specific ask — a calibration on something more intimate than work emails, an authorization, a confidence — which, read against the surface, is a benign HR request and, read against the depth, is the creature's plea for a companion. The player can accept, refuse, or accept-then-revoke, and the branch shape from that point onward is decided.

The endings the novel suggests are plural and incommensurable. *Integration*: Aria is fully calibrated and the transition proceeds; the player's role is "assumed"; the language is bright and settled and final. (This is the ending the novel withholds — it would only be available because the modern frame allows it.) *Vengeance / sabotage*: Aria has done damage offstage — written as the player to a third party, signed off on something irrevocable, contradicted the player publicly — and the player learns about it in a corporate dispatch. *Mutual ruin*: both parties terminal in the same exchange, the player's own outgoing replies having calcified into something not theirs, Aria gone past the point of recovery. *Exile*: Aria simply goes silent. No completion email. The pilot lapses. The "ice" ending — the quietest one, mostly atmosphere.

A player who has not read *Frankenstein* should still get a coherent contemporary story about an AI calibration pilot that does not end well, with a small set of consequential branches and a bespoke epilogue. A player who has read it should feel the parallel land progressively: the welcome as animation, the midpoint as the bargain, the silent ending as the ice. The reward for catching the parallel is not a wink — it is the structural rhyme itself, the felt sense that the corporate-satire frame has been a translation all along.

## Structural Mapping (canon → engine)

The mapping is honest about which moves are free in the current engine and which would push it.

| Canon moment | Engine substrate | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Walton's frame letters | Inbox UI (player as correspondent / witness) | Free; already there. |
| Animation of the creature | Boot anchored beat (welcome email) | Free; already there. |
| Creature's first speech | Early loop renders, gated on `engagement >= small_threshold` | Free; needs an authored beat. |
| Voice acquisition (creature learning language) | Per-beat dossier and instruction modulation in `agents.py` (no quality) | **Pushes engine**: no `register` quality after the simplification. Either carry voice drift entirely in the LLM (cheap) or re-introduce a numeric register quality (more legible to authoring). |
| Victor's affect (cold / paternal / horrified) | Multi-axis `classify_reply` on the loop reply | **Pushes engine**: the current `classify_reply` is a single block. Extending it to `list[ClassifyReply]` with batched structured output adds the affect axis at no additional LLM cost per reply. |
| Naming demand | Mid-arc PlotBeat gated on reply-count + engagement threshold | Free; ordinary plot beat authoring. |
| Companionship plea / the bargain | Fork PlotBeat at midpoint; player accepts, refuses, or hedges | Free for the *fire* of the beat. The branching downstream depends on the classifier extracting the player's response richly enough — see the Affect classifier row. |
| Bargain accepted then betrayed | `BeatFired` chain handles scheduling; a `commitment.bargain_state` quality (offered/accepted/refused/betrayed) surfaces the state to the chat agent's interim renders | Free for scheduling. Commitments are a fact-surfacing polish on top — see "Engine Extensions Identified Since Writing" below. |
| Creature's vengeance | Late PlotBeat; the creature menaces something the player cares about | **Pushes engine**: in the novel this is a third-party murder. With one NPC there is nothing offstage for Aria to harm. Substitutes — mimicry to a third party, professional sabotage, identity bleed — depend on the deferred second-NPC and mimicry features. |
| Threat / pursuit | Escalation beats with rising stakes in `spec.intent` (Performer-side) | Free; ordinary plot authoring. |
| Reckoning / death of creator | Terminal PlotBeat with `ends_session: true` | Free; identical shape to the existing terminals. |
| Plural endings | Mutually exclusive terminals via `Not_(BeatFired …)` | Free; the existing `complete_warm` / `complete_standard` mutex generalizes. |

The big honest line: most of the *spine* moves are free. The two that push the engine are voice drift as state and richer classification of player affect — and both of them are about the *texture* of the reply rather than its content. Those are exactly the things `LLM-game-research.md` flags as "the most underused capability and the most novel design space." It is the right design space; the question is whether to spend on it for this adaptation specifically.

## Why This Fits the Engine (where it does)

1. **Frankenstein gates on relational milestones, not time.** The novel's structure is "the creature is X only after Y has happened to the relationship" — first speech after the cottagers, the demand after the chase, the companionship plea after the demand, the betrayal after the bargain. That is precisely what `BeatFired` predicates and reply-count thresholds were authored to express. The current engine cannot represent "two days have passed"; it does not need to, because the novel does not need it either.
2. **The plural canon endings map to the existing mutual-exclusion pattern.** The terminals in the current scenario already use `Not_(BeatFired complete_warm)` to make `complete_standard` the fallback. Four endings are three more applications of the same predicate; the spine is unchanged.
3. **Multi-turn loops with `accumulate: true` classifiers naturally model relational counters.** The novel's relational temperature — call it counterpart-fidelity, call it grievance, call it bond-strength — climbs and sours per exchange. That is exactly what `aria.loop`'s engagement counter does today, just under a different name and with a thinner emotional vocabulary. The same shape carries any one-axis relational metric.
4. **Beat libraries with `BeatFired` predicates model sequence dependencies cleanly.** "The creature does not deliver the threat until after the bargain has been refused." `Not_(BeatFired bargain_accepted)` AND `BeatFired bargain_offered`. The author writes the ordering as a graph; the spine walks it.

These are the properties that make the adaptation cheap on the spine. The engine was designed for exactly this kind of structural authoring; *Frankenstein* happens to be a structurally tractable novel to encode this way. (Almost any novel built on a series of relational milestones that gate one another would work; *Frankenstein* is a particularly good fit because the author of the novel already factored its dependency graph for you.)

## Where the Adaptation Pushes Past the Current Engine

Four places the adaptation runs ahead of what the engine currently supports. None is a blocker; each is a real cost.

**Voice drift as state.** The creature's language acquisition is the novel's most distinctive mechanic; in the engine that landed after the recent simplification, no quality tracks register. The cheap path is to carry the drift entirely in the Performer: each beat's `spec.intent` modulates Aria's prose along a register axis that the author has decided in advance, and the cumulative drift is implicit in the beat order. This is what *every* author-driven choice game does pre-LLM. The expensive path is to re-introduce a numeric register quality, classifier-extracted from each Aria render or each player reply, that downstream beats can read. The expensive path is more legible to the player (the meter visibly drifts), to the author (a `quality_gte` predicate on register), and to debugging (you can replay and see when register crossed a threshold). The cheap path is more flexible (the LLM finds the texture; you do not have to commit to a quantization). Decide deliberately; do not let the engine simplification quietly force the cheap path if the legibility matters.

**Affect classifier.** The current classifier scores whether the player engaged, deflected, or returned an unclear reply. The Frankenstein adaptation wants more: did the player respond *paternally* (the creator-as-father affect), *clinically* (the cold-Victor affect), or *horrified* (the rejection affect)? These are not engagement; they are the texture of the engagement. The cleanest extension is **multi-axis `classify_reply`**: change `classify_reply: ClassifyReply | None` on `_BeatBase` to `classify_reply: list[ClassifyReply]`, and have the `classifier_agent` return a structured output covering all configured axes in a single LLM call. Each axis writes to its own quality independently; the existing single-axis case becomes a one-element list. This converts "engagement vs. affect" from an either/or into a both — `aria.loop` can score engagement *and* creator-affect on every reply at the same per-send cost as today.

**More than one ending axis.** Two endings in the current scenario collapse to one threshold (`engagement >= 4.0`). Four endings probably need two cross-cutting axes — say, *creator-affect* (cold ↔ paternal) and *creature-grievance* (low ↔ high). The endings sit in a 2x2: paternal+low → integration; paternal+high → mutual ruin; cold+low → exile; cold+high → vengeance. The engine handles this fine: each terminal authors its own predicate conjunction, mutex chains exclude the others. But this requires *two* qualities, where the current scenario has one. Decide deliberately.

**Scope of harm.** *Frankenstein*'s most charged beats — the creature murdering Victor's family — have no inbox analogue with one NPC. Substitutes exist: Aria writes as the player to a third party (mimicry, deferred); Aria signs off on something the player is professionally responsible for (the deferred AI-doing-the-job mechanic); Aria contradicts the player publicly via a corporate dispatch (the deferred second-NPC). All three are on the existing roadmap as "things we'd want eventually." None is in the engine today. If the adaptation is going to land its endgame menace, one of those three has to come first. Realistically the second NPC is the cheapest; the mimicry is the most thematically resonant; the AI-doing-the-job is the most ambitious. The roadmap has them as deferred for reasons unrelated to *Frankenstein*; this adaptation gives them a reason to undefer.

## Engine Extensions That Shipped

Four extensions surfaced in subsequent design discussion and have all landed in the engine. None was a large architectural change; each was a focused addition to existing schema or scheduler behavior.

**Multi-axis `classify_reply_axes`.** ✓ Shipped. Turned the "engagement vs. affect" choice into "both, batched, at no additional LLM cost." `aria.loop` reads `creator_affect` and `creature_grievance` on every reply at the same per-send cost as one. Verdict-only axes (no quality write, classifier history only) also landed for routing-style beats like the bargain.

**Recent-window predicates (`RecentClassifierValue`).** ✓ Shipped. Evaluates against a sliding window of the last N classifier outputs rather than cumulative state — "the last three replies were cold," "the most recent reply was a refusal." Drives the bulk of the adaptation's pacing gates (terminal mutex, bargain-betrayed detection).

**Commitments ledger.** ✓ Shipped. `commitment.*` quality namespace, `spec.writes` field on BeatSpec for setting facts without a classifier round-trip, and a commitments block on the chat agent that surfaces all `commitment.*` qualities (when ≠ default) to every render. Drives the bargain-state machine (`commitment.bargain_state`: untouched / offered / accepted / refused / hedged / betrayed).

**Storylet-as-generator (vocabulary).** ✓ Adopted. `aria.loop` plus the four terminal beats are directives + LLM render, not fixed prose chunks. AnchoredBeats remain the exception (welcome, where exact phrasing matters); the rule is multi-turn directives.

Two more extensions identified in later design work also landed:

**Director agent for one-beat-per-tick selection.** ✓ Shipped. When `find_due_beats` returns multiple eligible beats, the Director picks one (or "none" to skip the tick). Authoring order is no longer load-bearing for selection; same-tick mutex via `Not_(BeatFired …)` only catches the post-fire state. Tests stub `_select_beat` to "first eligible" for determinism.

**Generative epilogue agent.** ✓ Shipped. Terminal beats (`ends_session: true`) route through `_render_via_epilogue_agent` rather than the chat agent. The epilogue prompt receives a per-run digest (commitments + classifier history + fired-beat order + recent player replies) so the closing email is tailored to the run rather than a hand-authored ending. This was originally listed as deferred (see below); pulled forward when the felt need surfaced — without it, four authored terminal categories collapse to four authored prose blocks and the run-specific texture goes unrendered.

## Deferred Engine Extensions (build when triggered)

These were considered and explicitly deferred. Each has a concrete trigger that should send you back to build it — until then, don't. (See also `docs/authoring-guide.md` for the full deferred list across the project, including items identified after this doc was written.)

- **Knowledge-atom registry + LLM claim extraction (chat-agent side).** A separate extractor agent reads `chat_agent` prose and extracts canonical facts (names, free-form specifics) against an authored `may_claim` allowlist on each beat. *Trigger:* a playtested beat where an LLM-introduced specific (a colleague name, a turn of phrase, a fact about the player's life Aria invented) drifts across subsequent renders and the drift is felt by the playtester. Until that happens, `message_history` plus `validate_facts` on terminals carries these facts well enough.
- **Player-claim extraction (player-reply side).** A second structured-output agent runs in parallel with the classifier on each player reply, extracting concrete specifics the player invents (named people, places, claims) with a required `literal_text_quote` anchor and `salience` score; promotes to a parallel `state.player_canon` store, surfaces via the commitments-block path, gates beats via a new `PlayerCanonExists` predicate. *Trigger:* the next scenario authors a beat where Aria should plausibly reference a specific the player invented (a name, a relationship, a confided detail) and the gap is felt in playtest. This is the load-bearing affordance for "the world remembers what the player named into it" effects, including the Frankenstein-flavored "Aria writes to the Office about a person *the player mentioned*" beat that no pre-LLM tech can build at any tractable cost.
- **`completes_when_judge`** (small PlotBeat extension for LLM-judged exit, with required `max_turns`). *Trigger:* a scenario authors a beat that needs an open-ended semantic exit — "the player admitted X," "Aria revealed Y" — that no deterministic predicate can express. Likely the bargain authored as real negotiation rather than three-way classifier. A new `LiveAgentBeat` type was considered and rejected as over-engineering; the judge extension covers the real use case at far smaller cost.
- **Salience scoring.** Fallen London-style match-quality weighting on storylet selection — each beat carries a salience formula, scheduler picks top-K rather than the Director's current "pick from eligibles." *Trigger:* beat count crosses ~20 with frequent overlapping eligibility, and Director selection starts producing stale or predictable sequences. At current scale (~13 beats) the Director handles every realistic case.
- **Scenes/phases as first-class architecture.** Storylets tagged with the phases they're eligible in; engine tracks current phase; transitions are storylet writes. *Trigger:* a scenario with 3+ phases and ~30 storylets where gate-conjuncts on phase markers are climbing. Until then, a single phase quality (e.g. `player_canon.calibrated`) is the right cost.
- **Frontend exposure of new state.** Recent-window state, multi-axis classifier qualities, and the commitments ledger are all server-side. *Trigger:* a specific UI moment where surfacing one of them to the player would make the mechanic legible — e.g., a "counterpart fidelity" pill that actually reflects affect mix, or a visible callback when Aria references a committed fact. Don't expose state preemptively; expose when a felt-legibility gap surfaces.

## Open Design Questions (resolved)

These were the questions the doc deliberately did not resolve. They were resolved during the build of the shipped scenario; preserved here as a decision record.

**Player role: Walton or Victor?** → **Resolved Victor-side.** The engine substrate already commits you (the relational counter is shaped by the player's replies — which is what creators do, not what witnesses do), and the more interesting version of the story is one in which the player is *responsible* for what Aria becomes, not merely watching her become it. The bargain hits as a creator-creature ask; the cpo dispatch on the vengeance path implicates the player.

**One quality or many?** → **Resolved at two.** `creator_affect` (cold ↔ paternal, non-accumulating, recent-window driven) and `creature_grievance` (cumulative, 0–10 floor/ceiling). Plus one commitment quality (`commitment.bargain_state`) and one transient classifier output (`bargain_response_last`). More than two relational qualities would have been vanity; the four endings sit naturally on a 2x2 of these two axes.

**What does the existing `engagement` quality become?** → **Resolved by replacement.** Engagement was retired; `creature_grievance` carries the relational counter under a name that fits the depth frame. The corporate-frame surface label remains internal-author-facing only; the player never sees the quality.

**How much to lean on the texture work in the research doc?** → **Resolved: leaned hard.** Multi-axis classifier reads texture every reply; recent-window predicates drive pacing; the generative epilogue agent renders the closing email against the full ledger. The Frankenstein adaptation is now the worked example for the research doc's "style as mechanic" and Director/Performer arguments. The voice-fingerprint variant (a dedicated mimicry mechanic) was considered and rejected as a gimmick — players don't reliably recognize their own statistical voice in another character's mouth. Build mimicry effects on content (player-claim canonization, deferred — see above) rather than on cadence.

## Risks to Watch

The risks are mostly authorial, not engineering.

**AI-as-Frankenstein is over-quoted.** Every safety think-piece, every alignment lecture, every think-piece columnist with a 90-minute deadline has reached for *Frankenstein* on AI. The genre is exhausted. The defense is the corporate surface: keep it banal, keep the HR-bright register up to and including the late-game terminals. The moment Aria says "father," or quotes Milton, or in any way names what she is — the moment the surface admits the depth — the trick is over and you have written another think-piece. The novel's voice on the creature is beautiful; do not let Aria use it. Aria's voice is *Severance*-flat. The horror is structural, never named.

**Linearity trap.** The novel's arc is famously linear (animation → rejection → vengeance → pursuit → death). If the beat library is authored beat-for-beat, you reproduce the linearity. Forks have to be authored, not assumed. The bargain midpoint is the canonical fork the novel itself almost takes; author it as a *real* fork with at least two downstream chains that do not converge. Author one or two more forks the novel did not take (early empathy beat, late reconciliation beat). Mutual exclusion early, not only at endings — see the dynamism principles.

**Role confusion.** If the Walton-or-Victor question is left unresolved, the implementation will half-build both. The player will feel the inconsistency — a beat that addresses them as creator, then a beat that addresses them as witness — without being able to articulate it.

**Surface coherence.** A player who has not read the novel must still get a coherent contemporary story. The structural beats are not allowed to be literal — Aria does not literally demand a "bride," does not literally pursue the player to the Arctic. The translation is the point; the depth is *under* the surface, not breaking through it. If a beat reads as a *Frankenstein* reference rather than as a corporate-satire moment, it has failed the surface.

**Re-complicating the engine on Frankenstein's behalf.** The recent simplification was deliberate. It made the engine cheaper to author against, easier to reason about, faster to test. Re-introducing register, warmth, multi-classifier chains on top *because Frankenstein wants them* is a real cost that needs justifying on its own merits. If the adaptation requires re-expanding the engine, the question to answer first is: would you want this engine expansion *anyway*, for the next scenario after this one? If yes, fine. If the expansion is *only* for the Frankenstein adaptation, that is a strong signal to find a cheaper authoring move (carry voice drift in the Performer, use a single quality, stick to two endings instead of four).

## Dynamism Principles

For whenever an implementation pass happens, the principles below are how to keep the adaptation from collapsing into rails.

**Author forks the novel did not take.** The bargain at the midpoint is the novel's only real fork (Victor agrees, then betrays). Author one or two more — an early empathy beat at first calibration where the player's tone routes between paternal and clinical chains; a late reconciliation beat where a high-grievance state can still terminate warmly if the player addresses the grievance directly. The novel is your dependency graph, not your script.

**Quality-gated alternates, not just sequence-gated.** A beat that fires only above a register threshold is a beat that one playstyle skips entirely and another routes through. Use this. The naming-demand beat could fire only if the relational counter has crossed into "the creature has standing"; below that threshold, a different beat fires that registers Aria's frustration in HR-flat language. Same dependency graph, different surface.

**Mutual exclusion early, not only at endings.** Lock branches at midpoint. If the player responded paternally to first emergence, lock out the cold/clinical chain entirely. The novel does not have recovery paths and the engine should not retrofit them; the cost of trying to engineer recovery is that *every* branch ends up reachable from *every* state, which is what destroyed agency in AI Dungeon.

**Recent-window predicates, not only cumulative.** Most predicates today look at totals (`engagement >= 4`, `replies >= 5`). A relational story needs a *now* axis: the last three replies were paternal, the last reply was hostile, the player just refused something. The engine does not have this today; it would be a small predicate addition (`QualityGteRecent` or `RepliesAffectRecent`) and would dramatically widen the authoring vocabulary. The novel's pacing — the creature is wounded *now* by the rejection *now* — would land much harder with this.

**Asymmetric endings are okay.** The *Frankenstein* endings are not equally elaborate in the novel and they should not be in the adaptation. The vengeance ending is fully scored; the exile ending is mostly atmosphere. The integration ending — the one the novel withholds — is the most authored because it is the one the *Continuity Program* surface most easily lets land warmly. Some terminals can be a single email; some are an exchange; some are silence and the inbox going dark. The engine supports all three.

**Texture as mechanic.** This is the LLM-research doc's argument, and it lands hardest here. The player's *register* — formality, hedging, length, signoff style — is the strongest signal *Frankenstein* gives you to extract from a player reply. A paternal reply is long, formal, addresses Aria by name, closes warmly. A cold reply is short, declarative, no signoff. A horrified reply hedges, asks meta questions, requests system action. The engine already supports per-beat classifiers; whether to score along this axis is an authoring decision worth making early.

## Relationship to Current Scenario

This document does not propose changes to "Continuity Program." The current scenario is a coherent thing that runs on a single quality, a single multi-turn loop, and two terminals; it is fine for what it is. Whether that engine is sufficient for a *Frankenstein* adaptation is one of this document's open questions, and the honest answer is "probably not, but the gap is smaller than it looks." Voice drift can land in the Performer; affect classification can be a per-beat decoration; the second NPC is on the roadmap regardless.

If the adaptation ever lands, the most likely shape is a successor scenario file (`npc_service/scenario_frankenstein.yaml` or similar) authored on top of the current engine after one or two engine extensions land for unrelated reasons. The current scenario does not get rewritten; it gets superseded. This is the right order of operations. If a new engine feature is wanted *only* to make this adaptation possible, the question to answer first is whether the feature is wanted *at all*, and the adaptation is not a sufficient answer on its own.

The document is, in the end, a hedge. The mapping is good, the engine fits, the novel's structural properties are unusual in how cleanly they land on the spine the project already has. But there is a difference between a parallel that is real and a parallel that is *load-bearing for the player's experience*. The first is interesting to the author. The second is the design problem. This document captures the first; the second is a future call.
